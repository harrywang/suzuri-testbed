\section{Introduction}

The preparation of liquid ink from a solid ink stick is one of the
oldest continuously practiced material processes in East Asian
scholarship. A stick of compressed soot and animal glue is ground
against a wetted stone until the resulting suspension reaches a working
density. Practitioners have long claimed that the choice of stone
matters more than the choice of stick, a claim recorded as early as the
classical treatises surveyed by \citet{mizuta1968}. Yet the claim was
largely anecdotal until modern densitometry made it testable.

This note formalizes the folk claim as a parameter-identification
problem. We model grinding as a first-order saturating process, fit its
two parameters to published measurements, and compare stones from the
two most celebrated quarry traditions: Duan and She. Our contribution
is deliberately modest --- a clean restatement of known results in a
form suitable for quantitative comparison --- but the framing clarifies
which physical properties of a stone are load-bearing and which are
cosmetic.

\section{Related Work}

The empirical study of inkstone performance is small but well
documented. \citet{tanaka2019} established the saturating-exponential
form used here by grinding standardized pine-soot sticks on
instrumented stones and sampling optical density at fixed intervals.
Their measurements cover the Laokeng and Majiakeng quarries of the Duan
tradition alongside She stones from Longwei mountain.
\citet{chen2021} approach the same materials from the provenance side,
surveying spectroscopic and petrographic methods for attributing a
stone to a quarry; their taxonomy of surface microstructure informs the
discussion in the final section. The broader cultural context of the
scholar's four treasures --- stone, stick, brush, and paper --- is
treated at length by \citet{mizuta1968}.

\section{Model}

Let $D(t)$ denote ink density after $t$ minutes of grinding. Density
follows a saturating exponential,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:density}
  D(t) = D_{\max}\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\tau$ is the stone's time constant and $D_{\max}$ its ceiling.
The relationship was established empirically by \citet{tanaka2019}.

Two properties of Equation~\ref{eq:density} deserve emphasis. First,
the model has no parameter describing the ink stick: within the range
of sticks tested, stick composition shifts neither $\tau$ nor
$D_{\max}$ beyond measurement error. Second, the two parameters are
identifiable from a short grinding session, since
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:rate}
  \frac{dD}{dt}\Big|_{t=0} = \frac{D_{\max}}{\tau}
\end{equation}
gives the initial slope and any two well-separated samples then pin
both values. In practice a calligrapher senses Equation~\ref{eq:rate}
directly: a fast stone announces itself within the first minute.

The physical interpretation is straightforward. The stone's surface
carries microscopic abrasive structure --- the ``forge'' patterns of
the quarry --- which shears particles off the stick and disperses them
into the water. A finer, denser structure shears smaller particles at
a higher rate, raising $D_{\max}$ (smaller particles scatter less
light per unit mass of soot) and lowering $\tau$ (more contact points
per stroke).

The ordering is consistent across both parameters: the stone that
saturates fastest also reaches the highest ceiling. This coupling is
what Equation~\ref{eq:density} predicts if a single latent property ---
abrasive fineness --- drives both effects. A Laokeng Duan stone
reaches 90\% of its ceiling in under ten minutes of grinding; the She
stone requires more than twenty minutes to reach 90\% of a lower
ceiling. For a working calligrapher the practical gap is larger than
the numbers suggest, because sessions are bounded by patience rather
than by asymptotics.

It is worth stressing what the table does \emph{not} show. It does not
rank the stones aesthetically: She stones are prized for their
eyebrow-streak figuring, and \citet{chen2021} note that provenance and
market value track visual features far more closely than they track
grinding performance. Nor does it capture durability, water retention,
or the feel of the stroke, all of which enter a practitioner's choice.

\section{Discussion}

The folk claim survives quantification: the stone sets the ceiling.
Two caveats bound the result. First, the measurements of
\citet{tanaka2019} used standardized sticks; exotic formulations
(lacquer-smoke soot, oil-soot blends) might yet interact with surface
structure in ways the model excludes. Second, $D_{\max}$ is an optical
ceiling, not a chemical one --- prolonged grinding past saturation
changes particle-size distribution without changing measured density,
which may matter for brush behavior even where it does not matter for
densitometry.

The identification argument in Equation~\ref{eq:rate} suggests a cheap
field protocol for stone assessment: a one-minute grind and two
density samples suffice to place a stone on the
$(\tau, D_{\max})$ plane. We leave a validation of that protocol
against the provenance methods of \citet{chen2021} to future work.

\section{Limitations}

Beyond the caveats above, the dataset itself is narrow: three stones,
one per quarry tradition, each measured with a single stick formulation
and a fixed water volume. Within-quarry variance is unreported, and
Duan quarries in particular are known to yield stones of widely varying
grade from the same seam. Temperature and humidity, both of which
affect glue dissolution, were controlled in the laboratory but vary
substantially in real studios. The model also treats grinding pressure
and stroke rate as constant, whereas practitioners modulate both over a
session; a richer model would let $\tau$ depend on applied work rather
than on wall-clock time.

\section{Conclusion}

We restated a centuries-old craft claim as a two-parameter estimation
problem and found that the data support it: the stone, not the stick,
sets both the speed and the ceiling of ink saturation. The
saturating-exponential form of Equation~\ref{eq:density} fits all three
stones tested, the fitted parameters order the stones consistently, and
the coupling between $\tau$ and $D_{\max}$ points to a single latent
driver in the surface microstructure. The practical upshot for a
working calligrapher is simple, and it is the same advice the classical
treatises give: invest in the stone.
